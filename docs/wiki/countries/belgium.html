Belgium

Modified

September 23, 2026

Overview

This dossier integrates the 2026 Country Report and the Recommendation for a Council Recommendation on Country-Specific Recommendations, both issued by the European Commission on 2026-06-03, with the European Commission’s Debt Sustainability Monitor published on 2026-02-12 and the Spring 2026 Economic Forecast published on 2026-05-21. The OECD Economic Survey published on 2024-09-23 provides additional structural context. The evidence highlights slowing economic growth, driven by a global energy shock from the Middle East conflict, with real gross domestic product (GDP) projected to decelerate to 0.7% in 2026. Fiscal challenges persist, with the general government deficit stabilizing at 5.2% of GDP in 2026 and public debt rising to 112.8% by 2027 under the Spring 2026 forecast, or potentially reaching 137.4% by 2036 under the no-policy-change baseline of the Debt Sustainability Monitor. The reports underscore structural weaknesses in budgetary coordination, the activation of a national escape clause for defence spending, and significant refinancing needs.

Economic

C1* Growth (Growth)

C1-E Broken or dominated sample

No evidence in the sources yet.

C1-T Structural break in trend

  • C1-T, structural break in trend: The OECD projects real GDP growth at 1.2% in 2024 and 1.4% in 2025. Potential growth is estimated at 1.5% in 2024 and 1.4% in 2025. The output gap is projected to remain negative, at -1.4% in both 2024 and 2025.
  • C1-T, structural break in trend: The European Commission projects real GDP growth to decelerate to 0.7% in 2026, driven by weaker private consumption and external shocks. Growth in 2025 slowed due to heightened global uncertainty and a fall in exports.
  • C1*, context: Real GDP growth in 2025 was 1.0%. The European Commission Spring 2026 Forecast projects real GDP to grow by 0.7% in 2026 and 0.9% in 2027.
  • C1-T, structural break: GDP growth is expected to decelerate to 0.7% in 2026 and rebound to 0.9% in 2027. Private consumption weakens due to reduced purchasing power.
  • **C1*, context**: Real GDP growth is projected at 1.0% in 2025, 1.1% in 2026, and averaging 1.3% from 2028 to 2036.

C1-A1 Growth not thin-tailed or serially independent

  • C1-A1, growth not thin-tailed or serially independent: Energy supply disruptions and elevated price volatility due to geopolitical tensions (war in the Middle East) are cited as risks. Belgium adopted fiscal measures to mitigate high energy prices, expected to cost less than 0.1% of GDP in 2026.

C1-A2 Inherited competitiveness gap

  • C1-A2, Inherited competitiveness gap: Wage indexation has pressured core inflation and temporarily weighed on price competitiveness. The Conseil Central de l’Economie estimates the wage gap between Belgium and reference countries (the Netherlands, France, Germany) will be around 1.8% by the end of 2024, up from 0.9% in 2022. Unit labour cost growth has impacted export performance.
  • C1-A2, inherited competitiveness gap: Unit labour costs rose by 2.5% in 2024, compared to 4.9% in the euro area. Real wages were 2.0% higher in 2025 compared to 2019. The automatic wage indexation system and wage norms create rigidity, potentially impacting competitiveness.
  • C1-A2, inherited competitiveness gap: The document notes that Belgium has one of the lowest levels of business dynamism in the EU. The share of high-growth businesses is well below the EU average. Barriers to scaling up include high labour costs and size-dependent tax relief.

Fiscal

C3* Fiscal capacity and plan (Fiscal Capacity)

C3-E Estimator fails or rests on one episode

  • C3-E, Estimator fails or rests on one episode: Belgium has a history of large primary surpluses, reaching 6% of GDP in the early 2000s. However, current projections show the primary balance deteriorating from -2.5% of GDP in 2025 to unsustainable levels without reform.
  • C3-E, history lags policy: The S2 debt-stabilisation indicator signals high risk, requiring a 7.0% of GDP improvement in the SPB in 2027 to stabilize debt long-term. The S1 debt-reduction indicator shows medium risk, requiring a 5.9% of GDP effort to reduce debt to 60% by 2070.

C3-T History lags policy

  • C3-T, History lags policy: The European Commission’s new economic governance framework requires Belgium to submit a national medium-term structural budgetary plan. Belgium is the only Euro Area country without fully developed national multiannual fiscal planning. A credible medium-term consolidation strategy is needed to put debt on a downward path. The OECD estimates that putting the debt-to-GDP ratio on a declining path would require a primary balance improvement of 3.7 percentage points of GDP between 2025 and 2030.
  • C3-T, history lags policy: Belgium has adopted a medium-term fiscal structural plan for 2025-2029, committing to limit net expenditure growth and reduce the deficit below 3% of GDP. This plan implies an improvement of the structural primary balance of 2.4 percentage points of GDP between 2025 and 2029.
  • C3-T, history lags policy: The Council of the European Union activated the national escape clause for Belgium in July 2025 to facilitate higher defence spending, allowing deviation from maximum growth rates of net expenditure.
  • C3-T, history lags policy: The excessive deficit procedure for Belgium is held in abeyance based on the European Commission’s assessment of effective action. The country is adhering to a corrective path recommended by the Council to bring an end to the excessive deficit. Net expenditure growth in 2025 (3.7%) was above the recommended maximum (3.6%) but within the flexibility of the national escape clause for defence spending. Projected net expenditure growth for 2026 (2.0%) is below the recommended maximum.
  • C3-T, history lags policy: The general government deficit is projected to stabilize at 5.2% in 2026 and rise to 5.4% in 2027.
  • **C3*, context**: The structural primary balance (SPB) before cost of ageing is assumed at -2.3% of GDP from 2026 onwards. This level is low compared with past fiscal performance.
  • C3*, context: The structural primary balance is projected at -2.3% of GDP on average for 2026-2035. The fiscal consolidation space is 88% in the baseline scenario.

C3-A Default is chosen, not only suffered

  • C3-A, default is chosen, not only suffered: The document highlights high fiscal sustainability risks in the medium and long term. Public spending is among the highest in the EU at 54.2% of GDP. Persistently high spending requires a high tax burden, weighing on economic efficiency. Reforms to pension and long-term care systems are underway but delays exist (e.g., pension reform adoption delayed from Q4-2025 to 2026).

Other fiscal context

  • C3*, context: The general government deficit is projected to stabilize at 5.2% of GDP in 2026 and rise to 5.4% in 2027. Public debt is forecast to increase from 107.9% of GDP in 2025 to 112.8% in 2027. The rise in 2027 is driven by higher defence expenditure and interest payments.
  • C3*, context: The general government deficit increased from 4.4% of GDP in 2024 to 5.2% in 2025. It is projected to remain at 5.2% in 2026 and rise to 5.4% in 2027. The increase in 2025 was due to lower tax revenues and higher defence spending. The 2026-2027 increase reflects high primary deficits, weak growth, and rising interest payments.
  • C3*, context: The fiscal stance was contractionary by 0.3% of GDP in 2025 and is projected to be contractionary by 0.8% in 2026 and 0.6% in 2027.

Debt

R1* Refinancing need (Refinancing Need)

R1-E Components missing or distorted

  • R1-E, Components missing or distorted: Public debt is high at 105.2% of GDP in 2023 (Maastricht definition) and projected to rise to 110.0% in 2025. Debt is concentrated in the federal government but regions and communities account for an increasing share (16.6% of total public debt in 2022). Household debt is elevated, with residential mortgages comprising around 55% of GDP.
  • R1-E, components missing: Stock-flow adjustments were 1.5% of GDP in 2025 and 0.5% in 2026, but zero thereafter in the baseline.
  • R1-E, components missing: The debt-to-GDP ratio is projected to increase to 112.8% by 2027.

R1-T Financing behavior shifts

  • R1-T, financing behavior shifts: Defence expenditure increased to 1.4% of GDP in 2025 (up 0.5 percentage points from 2021) and is projected at 1.6% of GDP in 2026. This increase is accommodated by the national escape clause activated for 2025–2028.

R1-A Part of the debt not exposed to the market

  • R1-A, part of the debt not exposed to the market: The document notes that Belgium’s complex federal structure implies substantial vertical fiscal imbalance, with regions and communities largely funded by transfers.
  • R1-A, part of the debt not exposed to the market: Belgium has requested EUR 8.3 billion in EU loans under Security Action for Europe (SAFE) for defence procurement as part of the ReArm Europe Plan.

Other refinancing context

  • R1*, context: Public debt is increasing significantly, reaching 112.8% of GDP in 2027. The debt-to-GDP ratio is rising primarily due to high general government deficits.
  • R1*, context: General government debt increased from 103.9% of GDP at the end of 2024 to 107.9% at the end of 2025. It is projected to rise to 110.5% in 2026 and 112.8% in 2027. The 2025 increase was driven by a high primary deficit (+2.9 percentage points), a favourable snowball effect (-1.3 percentage points), and a debt-increasing stock-flow adjustment (+2.3 percentage points).
  • **R1*, context**: Gross debt is projected to increase to 137.4% of GDP by 2036. Gross financing needs are expected to rise from 19.7% of GDP in 2025 to 25.8% of GDP in 2036.
  • R1*, context: Gross financing needs were 19.7% of GDP in 2025 and are projected to remain elevated. Debt is projected to increase to 137.4% of GDP by 2036.

R6* Backstop eligibility (Backstop Eligibility)

Bright-line inputs

No evidence in the sources yet.

R6-A Bright lines versus real eligibility

  • R6-A, Bright lines versus real eligibility: The European Commission assesses Belgium’s fiscal trajectory under the new EU economic governance rules. Belgium’s deficit and debt levels are well above the thresholds for a reference trajectory (3% and 60% of GDP). The European Commission will present a reference path for annual growth in net primary expenditure to ensure debt follows a plausible downward path.
  • R6-A, bright lines versus real eligibility: Belgium is subject to an Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP), which is held in abeyance. The country has a record of compliance with net expenditure recommendations, but the EDP remains open.
  • R6*, context: The excessive deficit procedure is held in abeyance. The Council endorsed the national medium-term fiscal-structural plan for 2025–2029. The European Commission considers that Belgium has complied with its commitments in a satisfactory manner, though some reforms face implementation delays.
  • **R6*, context**: The document notes the activation of the national escape clause (NEC) for Belgium.

National institutions

  • High Council of Finance - Public Sector Borrowing requirements (HCF-PSBR): The High Council of Finance - Public Sector Borrowing requirements (HCF-PSBR) has been hampered by a lack of binding agreements on budgetary objectives and insufficient financial and human resources. Reforms are underway to redefine its tasks and enhance its capacity.
  • Budgetary Coordination: An agreement on enhanced budgetary coordination was reached in March 2026 to address structural weaknesses in the budgetary process, including a lack of multiannual fiscal planning at the national level and insufficient time for legal review.

Market Signals

  • Factor decoupling: Long-term government bond yields increased significantly, adding pressure on public finances. The 10-year government bond yield averaged 2.9% in 2024.
  • Factor decoupling: The report notes that Belgium’s tax wedge on labour is well above the EU average (50.8% vs 40% in 2024), which constrains work incentives.
  • Factor decoupling: The 10-year sovereign yield spread vs. German Bund was 51 bps at the end of 2025. Fitch downgraded the rating from AA- to A+ with a stable outlook in June 2025. S&P revised its AA outlook from stable to negative in April 2025.

Outlook Material

  • Elections: The latest general election was in June 2024.
  • Banking sector: The banking sector remains resilient with capital and liquidity ratios above regulatory requirements, although leverage is relatively high. Non-performing loans have increased moderately from record lows.
  • Climate risks: Belgium is vulnerable to energy supply disruptions and climate-related events such as flooding. Energy intensity is high.
  • Outlook Factors, institutional weakness: The budgetary process suffers from structural weaknesses, including a lack of multiannual fiscal planning at the national level and insufficient time for legal review.
  • Outlook Factors, pension reform: A draft law to reform the pension system was tabled in March 2026. It aims to reduce the projected increase in pension expenditure by 1.3 percentage points of GDP between 2024 and 2070 by introducing a bonus-malus system and phasing out favorable conditions for civil servants.
  • Outlook Factors, energy transition: Belgium remains highly dependent on fossil fuel imports, with fossil fuels accounting for 67.1% of the energy mix in 2024. The share of renewable energy in final energy consumption was 14.3% in 2024, the lowest in the EU.
  • Banking sector: The Belgian banking sector appears sound and profitable, with a stable average capital-adequacy ratio of 20.2% in Q3-2025. The non-performing-loan ratio slightly increased to 1.7% in Q3-2025.
  • Elections/Political Stability: The document does not explicitly discuss elections, but it highlights the complexity of coordination between federal, regional, and local authorities, which complicates policy delivery and reform implementation.
  • Structural Challenges: Long-term fiscal sustainability risks are driven by ageing-related spending, particularly pensions and long-term care. Projected ageing-related spending increases by around 5 percentage points of GDP by 2070. Pension reforms are expected to reduce this increase by 1.6 percentage points, but long-term care costs remain a concern.
  • Business Environment: High regulatory burden and administrative complexity impair the business environment. Simplification initiatives are ongoing, but permits are complex and costly, affecting construction and infrastructure projects.
  • Energy Transition: Belgium remains highly dependent on fossil fuel imports (67.1% of energy mix). Roll-out of renewables is held back by insufficient grid capacity and permitting issues. Fossil fuel subsidies amount to 2.4% of GDP (2022 estimate), with many lacking phase-out plans before 2030.
  • Climate Risk: Under the “Fragmented Action” climate scenario, debt could be around 3 pps. of GDP higher in 2050 compared to the baseline.
  • Political Risk: Lack of effective budgetary coordination between government levels is cited as a risk-increasing factor.
