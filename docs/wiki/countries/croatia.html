Croatia

Modified

September 23, 2026

Overview

This dossier draws on the European Commission Country Report and Country-Specific Recommendations published on 2026-06-03, the OECD Economic Survey published on 2026-01-30, the European Commission Debt Sustainability Monitor published on 2026-02-12, and the European Commission Economic Forecast published on 2026-05-21. The documents assess Croatia’s economic performance, noting strong but moderating real GDP growth driven by domestic demand, while inflation remains above the euro-area average. Key fiscal issues include a general government deficit exceeding the 3% of GDP threshold in 2025 due to rising expenditures on investments, wages, and pensions, alongside the activation of the National Escape Clause for defence spending. Structural challenges persist, including high unit labour costs, labor shortages, an inefficient housing market, and significant demographic headwinds from a shrinking and aging population.

Economic

C1* Growth (Growth)

C1-E Broken or dominated sample

No evidence in the sources yet.

C1-T Structural break in trend

  • C1-T, Structural break in trend: The OECD notes that economic growth is set to moderate but remain robust in 2026 and 2027. Real GDP growth is projected to decline from 3.8% in 2024 to 2.4% in 2027. This moderation reflects slower real disposable income growth moderating private consumption and the end of the EU Recovery and Resilience Facility funding, which will ease investment growth in 2027.
  • C1-T, Structural break in trend: The European Commission notes that wages grew faster than labour productivity in 2025, leading to a further appreciation of the real effective exchange rate and suggesting a loss of competitiveness. Unit labour costs increased significantly, growing by 11.3% in 2023 and 10.8% in 2024, with forecasts of an 8.9% increase in 2025.
  • C1-T, structural break: GDP growth is expected to moderate to 2.7% in 2026 and 2.5% in 2027. Domestic demand supports growth, but net exports weigh negatively.

C1-A1 Growth not thin-tailed or serially independent

  • C1-A1, context: Structural challenges weigh on growth potential, including labor and skills shortages, regulatory complexity, and administrative inefficiencies. Permitting processes are burdensome, and business-to-business payment periods are the longest in the EU.
  • C1-A1, context: The fragmented public research and innovation sector dilutes resources and hinders collaboration, resulting in lower research outputs and reduced scope for technology transfer. Public R&D expenditure as a share of GDP remains below the EU average and has been stagnating.

C1-A2 Inherited competitiveness gap

  • C1-A2, inherited competitiveness gap: Wage growth has exceeded productivity growth in recent years, raising concerns about cost-competitiveness. Unit labour costs have risen rapidly, partly mitigated by reduced profit margins, but service price inflation remains high and sticky due to strong wage growth and tourism demand.
  • C1-A2, Inherited competitiveness gap: Wage growth has outpaced productivity, contributing to higher unit labour costs. The European Commission warns that this persistent gap could weigh on future competitiveness, despite solid goods export growth.

Other growth context

  • C1*, context: The direct impact of recent trade tensions on the Croatian economy is limited, but the country could be indirectly affected by weakening demand from European trading partners, to which it directs about 65% of goods exports.
  • C1*, context: The European Commission’s Spring 2026 Forecast projects real GDP growth of 2.7% in 2026 and 2.5% in 2027, following actual growth of 3.4% in 2025.
  • C1-E, Context: Real GDP growth slowed to 3.4% in 2025 from 3.8% in 2024, but remains strong. Growth is projected to continue at decreasing rates. Domestic demand remains the primary driver, supported by rising incomes and employment.

C2-T Projections stale

  • C2-T, Projections stale: Administered price effects, such as the phase-out of energy support and the reintroduction of fuel price caps in March 2026 due to geopolitical tensions, create mechanical price impacts. The European Commission notes that food price caps have had limited long-term effectiveness in curbing inflation.

Other inflation context

  • C2*, Context: Inflation in Croatia reached 4.4% in 2025, firmly above the euro-area average of 2.1%. The gap was driven by services inflation, food inflation, and energy price increases due to the phase-out of government support measures.

Fiscal

C3* Fiscal capacity and plan (Fiscal Capacity)

C3-E Estimator fails or rests on one episode

No evidence in the sources yet.

C3-T History lags policy

  • C3-T, history lags policy: The fiscal stance has been expansionary despite a positive output gap and elevated inflation. The general government deficit target for 2025 was revised up to 2.9% of GDP from 1.9% in 2024, driven by hikes in public sector compensations, social benefits, and cost-of-living support measures.
  • C3-T, history lags policy: A tighter fiscal policy stance is warranted. The medium-term fiscal-structural plan aims to reach a primary structural deficit of 0.4% of GDP by 2028, implying a consolidation need of around 1.8% of GDP between 2025-2028. Further measures to reach these targets have not been specified.
  • C3-T, History lags policy: Croatia has activated the National Escape Clause under the EU fiscal framework to facilitate higher defence spending. Defence expenditure is projected to rise from 1.5% of GDP in 2024/2025 to 1.6% in 2026 and 1.7% in 2027.
  • C3-T, context: Net expenditure grew by 10.9% in 2025, exceeding the recommended maximum growth rate. The cumulative deviation for 2024-2025 was 1.4% of GDP, or 0.9% of GDP when accounting for defense spending flexibility under the national escape clause. Projected net expenditure growth for 2026 is 5.7%, leading to a cumulative deviation of 1.6% of GDP (1.0% with flexibility) for 2024-2026.
  • C3-T, context: The Council of the EU allowed Croatia to activate the national escape clause for defense spending in 2025-2028, permitting deviations from net expenditure ceilings to support higher defense expenditure.
  • C3-T, context: The fiscal stance was expansionary by 1.6% of GDP in 2025. It is projected to be contractionary by 0.3% of GDP in 2026 and 0.9% of GDP in 2027.
  • C3-T, context: Croatia adopted temporary fiscal measures to mitigate the impact of high energy prices following the war in the Middle East in February 2026, including excise duty reductions and targeted support. The projected fiscal cost is 0.1% of GDP in 2026, or 0.2% if measures remain in force until year-end.
  • C3-T, history lags policy: The general government deficit is projected to narrow from 3.0% in 2025 to 2.9% in 2026 and 2.7% in 2027.

C3-A Default is chosen, not only suffered

No evidence in the sources yet.

Other fiscal context

  • C3*, context: Long-term spending pressures from aging, the green transition, and defense are significant. Without further measures, public debt would rise to about 90% of GDP by 2040. A recent pension reform aims to increase pension adequacy but is estimated to increase pension expenditure by about 1 percentage point of GDP by 2040.
  • C3*, context: Tax revenues stood at 38.4% of GDP in 2024. Tax expenditures accounted for around 4.2% of GDP in 2022. The OECD recommends broadening the VAT base and phasing out inefficient tax expenditures.
  • C3*, context: The general government deficit increased from 2.3% of GDP in 2024 to 3.0% in 2025, driven by nationally financed investments, public wages, and social assistance. The European Commission projects the deficit to decrease to 2.9% in 2026 and 2.7% in 2027.
  • C3*, Context: The general government deficit widened to narrowly exceed the 3% of GDP reference value in 2025. This was driven by increased expenditure on public investments, wages, pensions, and social benefits. The European Commission projects a decrease to 2.9% of GDP in 2026 and 2.7% in 2027.
  • C3*, Context: The European Commission highlights that spending reviews are in early stages and have a narrow scope. The Fiscal Policy Commission has a narrow mandate and limited capacity. There is a call for improving the quality and efficiency of public spending.
  • C3*, context: The structural primary balance is projected at -1.7% of GDP on average for 2026-2035. The fiscal consolidation space is 55% in the baseline scenario.
  • C3*, context: The European Commission projects a structural primary deficit of 1.7% of GDP as of 2026. This is in line with past fiscal performance. Ageing-related expenditure is projected to decrease slightly.
  • C3*, context: The S2 indicator points to low risk, requiring a 1.5 percentage point improvement in the structural primary balance by 2027. The S1 indicator also points to low risk, requiring a 1.3 percentage point effort.

Debt

R1* Refinancing need (Refinancing Need)

R1-E Components missing or distorted

  • R1-E, components missing or distorted: Public debt continued to decline and fell below the Maastricht threshold of 60% of GDP in 2024 (57.4%), driven by robust growth and elevated inflation. Net financial debt stood at 31.0% of GDP in 2024.
  • R1-E, components missing: The debt-to-GDP ratio is foreseen to decline to 55.6% by 2027.

R1-T Financing behavior shifts

  • R1-T, context: The financing landscape is predominantly bank-supported, with market-based finance playing a smaller role due to low retail participation and limited domestic institutional investor involvement in innovative businesses.

R1-A Part of the debt not exposed to the market

  • R1-A, part of the debt not exposed to the market: Contingent liabilities and fiscal risks, such as those stemming from state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and climate change, are assessed. SOEs accounted for assets worth around 45% of GDP in 2021.
  • R1-A, Part of the debt not exposed to the market: Croatia secured EUR 1.7 billion in loans under the SAFE initiative for defence procurement, supported by the EU. Additionally, significant resources are channeled through the Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRP) and Cohesion Policy funds, which support public investment.
  • R1-A, context: The net international investment position (NIIP) adjusted for non-defaultable instruments reached +23.8% of GDP in Q3 2024. Financing sources are broadly stable.

Other refinancing context

  • R1*, context: The financial sector appears resilient, but rapid credit growth poses risks. Household indebtedness is internationally low at 30.2% of GDP in 2024, but new loans were disbursed with looser lending standards in 2024. Real house prices increased by close to 66% between the second quarter of 2015 and the second quarter of 2025.
  • R1*, context: General government debt decreased from 57.4% of GDP at the end of 2024 to 56.3% at the end of 2025. The European Commission projects a further decline to 55.9% in 2026 and 55.6% in 2027.
  • R1*, Context: General government debt-to-GDP is expected to decrease from 56.3% in 2025 to 55.6% by end-2027. Public investment remains supportive of GDP growth, reaching 6.4% of GDP in 2026.
  • R1*, context: Gross financing needs were 12.6% of GDP in 2025. Debt is projected to increase to 68.4% of GDP by 2036.
  • R1*, context: Gross financing needs are expected to amount to just above 12% of GDP over 2026-2027, reaching 14% of GDP in 2036.

Institutions

R6* Backstop eligibility (Backstop Eligibility)

Bright-line inputs

  • R6*, context: Croatia meets both the EU debt (60% of GDP) and deficit (3% of GDP) criteria. The European Commission provided a “technical information” at the country’s request, aligning with the medium-term fiscal-structural plan.
  • R6*, context: Croatia was not identified in the 2026 Alert Mechanism Report as requiring an in-depth review under Regulation (EU) No 1176/2011.
  • R6*, Context: Croatia is subject to the EU fiscal framework. The Council activated the National Escape Clause for defence spending. The European Commission assesses compliance with net expenditure growth rates, noting partial explanation by the NEC flexibility.
  • R6*, context: The Council of the EU endorsed Croatia’s national medium-term fiscal-structural plan for 2025-2028, which sets maximum growth rates for net expenditure.

R6-A Bright lines versus real eligibility

  • R6-A, bright lines versus real eligibility: The perception of corruption among citizens and businesses remains very high (92% of respondents consider it widespread). This poses risks to public sector efficiency and public integrity.
  • R6-A, Bright lines versus real eligibility: The document notes challenges in the justice system efficiency and high perception of corruption, which may affect institutional quality assessments. However, formal compliance with EU fiscal rules is being monitored with specific allowances for defence.

National institutions

The European Commission highlights that the Fiscal Policy Commission has a narrow mandate and limited capacity. The document also notes challenges in the justice system efficiency and high perception of corruption.

Market Signals

  • Factor decoupling, rollover pessimism: The ten-year government bond yield is projected to remain stable around 3.0-3.2% through 2027.
  • Factor decoupling, context: The document does not provide specific bond spread data but notes that Croatia has the sixth-highest wholesale electricity prices in the EU, which impacts industrial competitiveness.
  • Outlook Factors, context: Croatia had the sixth highest wholesale electricity price in the EU in 2025, affecting cost competitiveness.
  • Rollover pessimism: The 10-year sovereign yield spread vs. German Bund is 33 basis points as of 31 December 2025.

Outlook Material

  • Demographics: Croatia’s population is shrinking and aging rapidly. The working-age population is projected to decline by about 25% between 2024 and 2050. The old-age dependency ratio is projected to increase from around 40% in 2024 to 59% in 2050.
  • Housing: High vacancy rates (around 15%) and short-term holiday rentals (around 10%) limit the housing stock for residential purposes. Real house prices and rents have increased rapidly in the past decade.
  • Climate: Croatia has significantly reduced greenhouse gas emissions since 2007 but needs additional measures to reach net-zero by 2050. Investment needs for the energy sector are estimated at EUR 45.5 billion (58% of 2023 GDP) to meet 2030 targets.
  • Geopolitical shocks: The conflict in the Middle East is expected to weigh on the macroeconomic outlook, dampening domestic demand. Croatia reintroduced fuel price caps in March 2026 in response to soaring oil prices.
  • Economic security: Croatia faces challenges related to economic security, including dependency on fossil-fuel imports and material import dependency above the EU average.
  • Regional disparities: Significant disparities persist in income, productivity, and public service provision between the capital region and less developed areas like Panonska Hrvatska.
  • Structural reforms: The document recommends consolidating public research institutes, simplifying regulation, merging local administration functions, and accelerating broadband deployment in rural areas.
  • Energy transition: Recommendations include accelerating renewable energy deployment, upgrading electricity grids, phasing out fossil fuel subsidies, and improving energy efficiency in buildings and transport.
  • Social challenges: The document highlights high poverty and income inequality, particularly among older people and those with disabilities, and calls for better targeting of social benefits and improved access to long-term care and healthcare.
  • Labor market: Persistent labor and skills shortages are addressed through recommendations to remove participation obstacles, strengthen education, and upskill workers.
  • Climate resilience: With low insurance coverage against climate risks, the document recommends creating incentives for climate risk insurance and investing in climate-proof infrastructure.
