Bulgaria

Modified

September 23, 2026

Overview

This dossier draws on the 2026 Country Report and the 2026 Recommendation for a Council Recommendation, both issued by the European Commission on 2026-06-03, and the 2026 Economic Survey issued by the OECD on 2026-02-05. New evidence incorporates the European Commission’s Debt Sustainability Monitor from 2026-02-12 and the Spring 2026 Economic Forecast from 2026-05-21. The reports highlight that Bulgaria joined the euro area in January 2026 and faces rising fiscal deficits and public debt. Key themes include an expansionary fiscal stance driven by defense expenditure under the national escape clause and social spending, leading to a proposed excessive deficit procedure. Structural challenges persist in tax collection, public administration, and the independence of the Supreme Judicial Council. The OECD and European Commission assessments note that while public debt remains low, long-term pressures from an ageing population and near-term deficits require consolidation. The Spring 2026 forecast projects growth deceleration due to a global energy shock.

Economic

C1* Growth (Growth)

C1-E Broken or dominated sample

No evidence in the sources yet.

C1-T Structural break in trend

  • C1-T, Structural break in trend: Real GDP growth reached 3.1% in 2025, driven by strong domestic demand including private consumption, investment, and public consumption. Private consumption was driven by rising real wages. Investment grew by 11.4%, supported by high credit volumes and increased absorption of Recovery and Resilience Facility funds.
  • C1*, context: Real GDP growth in 2025 was 3.1%. The Commission Spring 2026 Forecast projects real GDP to grow by 2.5% in 2026 and 2.2% in 2027 .
  • C1*, context: Real GDP growth returned to its pre-pandemic average rate of 3% in recent years, driven by strong consumption growth supported by robust wage increases and credit growth. Growth is projected to moderate to 2.6% in 2026 as the economy rebalances toward investment.
  • C1-T, structural break in trend: The OECD notes that even at current growth rates, it would take 28 years for Bulgaria to converge to average OECD income levels. Structural reforms (investment, skills, productivity) are estimated to raise per capita GDP by 2.3% in the next ten years and 9% by 2060.
  • C1-T, structural break: Real GDP growth is forecast to decelerate to 2.5% in 2026 and 2.2% in 2027. Domestic demand growth slows.
  • **C1*, context**: Real GDP growth is projected at 3.0% in 2025, 2.7% in 2026, and averaging 2.3% from 2028 to 2036.

C1-A1 Growth not thin-tailed or serially independent

No evidence in the sources yet.

C1-A2 Inherited competitiveness gap

  • C1-A2, Inherited competitiveness gap: Nominal unit labour costs rose by 9.4% in 2025, mostly driven by increases in real wages. Wage growth stood at 10.4% in 2025, following increases of more than 13% between 2022 and 2024. This growth is well above the European Commission average and outpaces labour productivity growth. Unit labour costs remain among the lowest in the EU, but the rapid growth raises competitiveness concerns.
  • C1-A2, inherited competitiveness gap: Unit labour costs (ULC) have tripled since EU accession, surpassing cost growth in other EU competitor countries. This has eroded the country’s cost advantage, as manufacturing is positioned at low value-added segments of global value chains.

Other growth context

  • C1*, context: The labour market remains tight with an unemployment rate of 3.5% in 2025. Labour market tightness, measured by the ratio of vacancies to unemployed workers, is at historically high levels. Labour shortages are acute in healthcare, industry, and construction.
  • C1*, context: R&D expenditure remains low. Public R&D spending was 0.27% of GDP in 2024, well below the EU average of 0.72%. Business R&D expenditure was 0.50% of GDP in 2024, stagnant and below the EU average of 1.49%.
  • C1*, context: Labour productivity growth slowed since the pandemic. The manufacturing sector, which accounts for around 15% of the economy, has seen negative average quarterly growth since 2023 due to higher energy prices and unit labour costs.
  • C1*, context: Bulgaria joined the euro area in January 2026, replacing the longstanding currency board. This should reduce costs and strengthen integration, but a temporary increase in inflation from price resets is a risk.

Other inflation context

  • Executive Summary: Inflation rebounded slightly in 2025, influenced by energy prices and tax changes, while nominal unit labor costs rose sharply.
  • C2*, context: HICP inflation stood at 3.5% in 2025. The Commission Spring 2026 Forecast projects HICP inflation to stand at 4.2% in 2026 and 2.6% in 2027 .
  • Topic: Euro adoption risks: A short-term spike in inflation may follow the euro’s introduction due to price resetting. The authorities have committed to active surveillance to avoid inflation “rounding-up” effects.

Fiscal

C3* Fiscal capacity and plan (Fiscal Capacity)

C3-E Estimator fails or rests on one episode

  • C3-E, history lags policy: The S2 indicator points to low risk, requiring only a 1.4% of GDP improvement in 2027 to stabilize debt. The S1 indicator also points to low risk, requiring 0.7% of GDP effort to keep debt below 60% by 2070.

C3-T History lags policy

  • C3-T, History lags policy: The general government deficit is projected to increase from 3.5% of GDP in 2025 to 4.1% in 2026 and 4.3% in 2027. This increase is driven by social spending, increases in public sector salaries, and planned defence expenditure under the national escape clause. Large and permanent increases in expenditure without compensatory structural financing measures have contributed to continued deficits.
  • C3*, context: The general government deficit increased from 3% of GDP in 2024 to 3.5% of GDP in 2025. The Commission Spring 2026 Forecast projects a deficit of 4.1% of GDP in 2026 and 4.3% of GDP in 2027 .
  • C3*, context: The fiscal stance was expansionary by 2.3% of GDP in 2025. It is projected to be expansionary by 1.0% of GDP in 2026, and contractionary by 1.6% of GDP in 2027 .
  • C3*, context: Net expenditure in Bulgaria grew by 12.3% in 2025, which is above the recommended maximum growth rate. Net expenditure is projected to grow by 5.5% in 2026, and 18.5% cumulatively over 2025 and 2026 .
  • C3*, context: A fiscal deficit emerged following recent crises and unfunded spending increases. The general government balance reached -3.0% of GDP in 2024, down from a surplus in 2019.
  • C3-T, history lags policy: The government’s medium-term budget plan relies heavily on higher tax collection to fund increases in pensions, public salaries, and investment, aiming to maintain the deficit around 3%. The OECD recommends front-loaded consolidation to narrow the deficit and maintain the current debt ratio.
  • C3-T, history lags policy: The government deficit is set to exceed 4% of GDP, reaching 4.1% in 2026 and 4.3% in 2027.
  • **C3*, context**: The SPB before cost of ageing is assumed at -1.7% of GDP from 2026 onwards. This is low compared with past performance.

C3-A Default is chosen, not only suffered

No evidence in the sources yet.

Other fiscal context

  • C3*, context: Bulgaria committed to a structural primary deficit target of 1.8% of GDP by 2028. The fiscal strategy aims to deliver commitments on net expenditure mainly through discretionary revenue-increasing measures with short-term impacts.
  • C3*, context: Total tax revenues amounted to 30.5% of GDP in 2024, well below the EU average of 39.4%. The tax system relies heavily on consumption taxes. The flat-rate personal income tax system limits redistributive capacity.
  • C3*, context: Outstanding tax arrears increased from 2019 to approximately 60% of total revenue collected at the end of 2023, more than double the EU average of 31%. The shadow economy accounts for almost 35% of GDP.
  • C3*, context: Bulgaria faces challenges in tax collection, with high levels of tax arrears and a large estimated shadow economy, which undermines tax compliance and limits revenue capacity .
  • C3*, context: Long-term fiscal pressures stem from an ageing population, with the old-age dependency ratio projected to increase from 38% in 2024 to around 60% by 2070. The pension system deficit reached 5.4% of GDP in 2024.
  • C3*, context: Defence spending stood at 1.3% of GDP in 2024 and is projected to rise to around 2.5% over the next three years, with a commitment to reach 5% of GDP by 2035.
  • C3*, context: Widespread labour informality results in significant fiscal losses. The National Revenue Agency estimated an average annual revenue loss of BGN 420 million in 2023 from underreported wages alone. Broader estimates suggest total revenue losses of 2.2% of GDP from 2017 to 2021.

Other refinancing context

  • R1*, context: Public debt is expected to increase from 29.9% of GDP in 2025 to 32.3% in 2026 and 35.5% in 2027, driven by the structural deficit.
  • R1*, context: Defence spending increased in 2024 and 2025, utilizing the national escape clause and Security Action for Europe (SAFE) loans. The Council of the EU made financial assistance available for up to EUR 3.262 billion.
  • R1*, context: General government debt increased from 23.8% of GDP at the end of 2024 to 29.9% of GDP at the end of 2025. The Commission Spring 2026 Forecast projects the debt-to-GDP ratio to increase to 32.3% by the end of 2026 and to 35.5% by the end of 2027 .
  • R1*, context: The increase in the debt ratio in 2025 mainly reflects capital injections into the Bulgarian Energy Holding and the Bulgarian Development Bank .
  • R1*, context: General government gross debt was 34.1% of GDP in 2024 and is projected to rise to 37.4% in 2026. Under the Maastricht definition, debt was 25.4% of GDP in 2024.
  • R1*, context: Household credit has grown strongly, with bank loans to households up by 21% in the year to November 2025. Mortgage lending has been fuelled by low interest rates, associated with soaring housing prices.
  • R1*, context: The banking system is well-capitalised and liquid. The liquidity coverage ratio is more than three-and-a-half times higher than required. However, the share of non-performing loans (NPL) remains relatively high at 3% as of Q3 2025.
  • Refinancing Need: The OECD notes that debt dynamics are currently favourable as expected nominal growth is well above interest rates on government debt.
  • **R1*, context**: Gross debt is projected to increase to 40.4% of GDP by 2036. Gross financing needs are stable around 5% of GDP.

Institutions

R6* Backstop eligibility (Backstop Eligibility)

Bright-line inputs

  • R6*, context: Bulgaria adopted the euro in January 2026. The European Commission assesses that Bulgaria may face challenges meeting its commitments under the medium-term fiscal-structural plan in the absence of robust consolidation efforts.
  • R6*, context: The European Commission has proposed to open an excessive deficit procedure for Bulgaria, recommending the Council adopt a Decision establishing the existence of an excessive deficit due to non-compliance with the deficit criterion in 2026 .
  • R6*, context: Bulgaria has activated the national escape clause for defense expenditure (2025-2028), allowing deviation from recommended maximum growth rates of net expenditure .
  • R6*, context: Bulgaria joined the euro area on 1 January 2026, following positive assessments from the European Commission and the European Central Bank, and decisions by ECOFIN and the EU Parliament.
  • Backstop Eligibility: With euro adoption, Bulgaria is now part of the European Stability Mechanism (ESM), providing additional financial protection. It is also fully integrated into the European banking framework subject to supervisory standards.
  • **R6*, context**: The document notes the activation of the national escape clause (NEC) for Bulgaria.

R6-A Bright lines versus real eligibility

  • R6-A, Bright lines versus real eligibility: Concerns about the independence of regulatory bodies and the Supreme Judicial Council persist. The Supreme Judicial Council has operated with an expired mandate since 2022. Anti-corruption measures, particularly in high-level cases, have not improved sufficiently.
  • R6-A, bright lines versus real eligibility: The OECD notes that managing economies without independent monetary policy is challenging. While Bulgaria has been in a similar position under its currency board, the transition requires prudent management of public finances to control domestic demand.

Bank–sovereign nexus and captive creditors

  • Banking Sector: Non-performing loans in the banking sector are cited as a risk-increasing factor.

Willingness to pay and political stability

  • Political stability and rule of law: Strengthening the prosecution of corruption remains key. Bulgaria has not yet set a robust track-record in investigating and prosecuting high-level corruption cases. The independence and effective functioning of the Supreme Judicial Council are essential, as it has operated with an expired mandate since 2022 .
  • Outlook Factors, political stability: A period of political instability with seven early elections in four years ended with a government forming in January 2025, which resigned in December 2025. This instability has delayed reforms and disbursement of EU funds.
  • Outlook Factors, rule of law: Corruption remains a critical issue. The OECD estimates that reducing corruption to Visegrad-4 levels would raise per capita GDP by 2.1% in the long run. Public procurement is identified as a high-risk area for state capture.

Market Signals

  • Outlook Factors: The introduction of the euro in January 2026 is expected to lower reserve requirements for commercial banks, potentially fueling credit growth. However, euro area membership will also broaden Bulgarian banks’ access to EU-wide capital markets, which could divert funds away from domestic lending.
  • Factor decoupling: Due to the currency board arrangement and low government debt, long-term government interest rates have largely tracked those in the euro area. Spreads to Germany have risen recently from near-zero levels, reflecting perceived political risk.
  • Rollover pessimism: Mortgage and deposit rates in Bulgaria have diverged from the euro area during the tightening cycle, remaining lower due to high liquidity in the domestic banking system. This has contributed to negative real rates and a housing boom.
  • Factor decoupling: The 10-year sovereign yield spread vs. German Bund was 112 bps at the end of 2025.

Outlook Material

  • Structural Challenges: Significant regional disparities persist. Economic activity and employment are heavily concentrated in urban areas, particularly the capital, which generates about half of national GDP. The north of the country remains structurally weak.
  • Energy: Wholesale electricity prices were among the highest in the EU in 2025, reflecting reliance on fossil fuels. Renewables surpassed coal in the electricity mix for the first time in 2025. Energy poverty remains widespread.
  • Public Administration: The public administration faces operational and capacity challenges, particularly at regional and municipal levels. The workforce is ageing, and municipalities struggle to attract and retain talent.
  • Justice: The justice system performs efficiently overall, but digitalisation gaps remain. Political independence of regulatory bodies is limited, and the Parliament plays a direct role in appointing regulators.
  • Energy security: Bulgaria adopted fiscal policy measures to mitigate the impact of high energy prices on households and firms, with a projected fiscal cost of 0.1% of GDP in 2026 if measures remain in force until end-2026 .
  • Topic: Climate risks: Bulgaria is vulnerable to extreme weather events such as floods and droughts. Insurance coverage against catastrophic risks remains extremely limited, with only 2% of all insurance contracts covering climate-related risks.
  • Topic: Demographics: The working-age population is shrinking rapidly. Net migration has turned positive since 2020, but emigration of skilled labour remains a serious issue.
  • Fiscal Capacity: The OECD projects that without policy adjustments, the government debt ratio (Maastricht definition) would rise to 160% by 2060 due to current budget deficits and ageing pressures. A consolidation scenario assumes an improvement in the primary fiscal balance by 0.5% of GDP per year over 2027-2033.
  • Climate Risk: Debt deviations under climate scenarios are generally small (below 1.5 pps. of GDP).
