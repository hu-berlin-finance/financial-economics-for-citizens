Finland

Overview

This dossier draws on the OECD Economic Survey (published May 2025), the European Commission Debt Sustainability Monitor (published February 2026), the European Commission Economic Forecast (Spring 2026, published May 2026), and the European Commission Country Report and Country-Specific Recommendations (published June 2026). The evidence highlights a fragile economic recovery with modest growth projections for 2026 and 2027. Fiscal sustainability remains a core challenge, with public debt projected to rise significantly to over 90% of gross domestic product by 2027, driven by increased defence spending, aging demographics, and structural deficits. The European Commission has opened an excessive deficit procedure (EDP), which is held in abeyance. Long-term fiscal sustainability risks are assessed as medium, requiring a structural primary balance improvement of 4.1 percentage points of gross domestic product to stabilize debt.

C1* Growth (Growth)

C1-E Broken or dominated sample

No evidence in the sources yet.

C1-T Structural break in trend

  • C1-T, structural break in trend: The real gross domestic product contracted by 0.1% in 2024 after a sharper decline in late 2023. Growth is projected to recover modestly to 0.7% in 2025 and 1.1% in 2026, supported by declining interest rates and easing inflation, but this recovery lags behind Nordic peers and the OECD average .
  • C1-T, Structural break in trend: The European Commission states that Finland’s economy is emerging from stagnation and is expected to transit to broad-based growth in 2026. After a decline in 2023 and a small rebound in 2024, real GDP grew only marginally in 2025. Subdued performance is attributed to declining private and public consumption and low consumer confidence .
  • C1*, context: Real gross domestic product (GDP) growth was 0.2% in 2025. The European Commission Spring 2026 Forecast projects real GDP to grow by 0.8% in 2026 and 1.4% in 2027 .
  • C1-T, structural break: Real GDP growth is projected at 0.8% in 2026 and 1.4% in 2027 .
  • C1*, context: Real GDP growth is projected to accelerate to 0.8% in 2026 and 1.4% in 2027, driven by domestic demand. In 2025, growth was modest at 0.2%. Private consumption is expected to increase by 0.6% in 2026, supported by rising disposable income from significant wage increases (8% total for 2025-27). However, the household savings rate remains high (12.4% in 2026) due to weak consumer confidence and high unemployment. Investment is set to rise in 2026, driven by machinery and equipment spending, including the delivery of F-35 fighter jets and data centre construction. Net exports are forecast to turn negative in 2026 due to import growth outpacing exports .

C1-A1 Growth not thin-tailed or serially independent

  • C1-A1, growth not thin-tailed: The recovery is fragile with downside risks skewed by geopolitical tensions, cyber-attacks, and trade policy uncertainty. A further escalation in global trade tensions or hybrid warfare could weaken export performance and delay investment .

C1-A2 Inherited competitiveness gap

  • C1-A2, Inherited competitiveness gap: Cost competitiveness has improved, with average wages relative to output per worker reaching their most advantageous levels since 2020. However, export market shares have contracted since 2020, indicating challenges with non-cost competitiveness such as low economic activity in traditional export destinations and a lack of new innovative products .
  • C1-A2, productivity gap: Despite strong innovation performance and high research and development expenditure, productivity growth has remained weak since the decline of the electronics sector in the early 2010s. The document notes that research results need to be commercialized more widely across the economy to contribute effectively to growth .

Other growth context

  • C1*, context: Productivity growth has weakened significantly, falling faster than in Nordic neighbors. This is attributed to the collapse of Nokia, the end of trade with Russia, and firms remaining too small .
  • **C1*, context**: Labour productivity growth has been limited since 2010, oscillating around zero following the collapse of the electronics sector. Productivity in the services sector has remained constant while growing in peer countries .

C3* Fiscal capacity and plan (Fiscal Capacity)

C3-E Estimator fails or rests on one episode

No evidence in the sources yet.

C3-T History lags policy

  • C3-T, history lags policy: The government announced consolidation measures in 2024, including tax increases and spending cuts. However, policy changes in April 2025, including income and corporate tax reductions and increased defense spending to 3% of gross domestic product by 2029, are estimated to reduce the fiscal balance by 1.7 percentage points of gross domestic product by 2029, slowing the pace of consolidation .
  • C3-T, History lags policy: The European Commission reports that an excessive deficit procedure (EDP) was opened in January 2026. Finland activated the ‘national escape clause’ to accommodate planned increases in defence expenditure. The Ministry of Finance estimates that stabilising public finances will require a consolidation effort of EUR 8–11 billion (2.7%–3.7% of GDP) over the next parliamentary term (starting 2027) .
  • C3-T, adopted consolidation: The Council of the EU recommended maximum growth rates of net expenditure of 1.3% in 2026, 1.5% in 2027, and 1.8% in 2028 to bring an end to the excessive deficit situation. The excessive deficit procedure is held in abeyance based on effective action .
  • C3-T, escape clause deviation: Projected net expenditure growth for 2026 is 4.1%, which exceeds the recommended maximum growth rate of 1.3% by 1.5% of GDP in annual terms. However, this deviation is within the flexibility of the national escape clause activated for defense spending .
  • C3-T, history lags policy: The general government balance is projected at -4.5% in 2026 and -4.6% in 2027 .

Other fiscal context

  • C3*, context: The general government fiscal balance deteriorated to -4.4% of gross domestic product in 2023 and is projected to improve slightly to -3.7% in 2025 and -3.2% in 2026 .
  • C3*, context: A structural gap between revenue and expenditure has existed since the Global Financial Crisis. Ageing-related costs are projected to rise considerably, with pension, health, and long-term care expenditure increasing from around 15.5% of gross domestic product to around 18.5% by 2040 .
  • **C3*, context**: The general government deficit declined to 3.4% of gross domestic product in 2025 but is projected to rise to 4.5% in 2026 and 4.6% in 2027. Public debt is expected to increase from 88.5% in 2025 to 93.1% in 2027. The government implemented consolidation measures in 2024–2025, including spending cuts and tax increases, but these did not suffice to stabilise the debt ratio .
  • **C3*, context**: A new fiscal framework was approved in December 2025, introducing a long-term debt anchor of 40% of gross domestic product and targets for four-year and eight-year cycles. The Economic Policy Council was designated as the independent fiscal institution responsible for monitoring compliance with European Union fiscal rules .
  • C3*, context: The general government deficit decreased from 4.4% of gross domestic product in 2024 to 3.4% of gross domestic product in 2025. The European Commission projects the deficit to rise to 4.5% of gross domestic product in 2026 and 4.6% of gross domestic product in 2027, driven by higher government investment in defense and interest payments .
  • C3*, high expenditure share: Public expenditure was 57.7% of gross domestic product in 2024, the highest in the Union. Social protection expenditure was 26% of gross domestic product in 2024, well above the Union average of 19% of gross domestic product. The document states that public debt is not expected to stabilize without additional interventions and calls for spending reviews to improve efficiency .
  • **C3*, context**: The SPB is assumed to be a deficit of 0.9% of gross domestic product as of 2026, excluding changes in the cost of ageing. This level is low compared with past fiscal performance .
  • **C3*, context**: Long-term fiscal sustainability risks are medium. The S2 indicator signals that Finland would need to improve its structural primary balance by 4.1 percentage points to ensure debt stabilizes over the long term .
  • **C3*, context**: The S1 indicator shows that a fiscal effort of 3.1 percentage points would be needed to reduce debt to 60% of gross domestic product by 2070 .
  • C3*, context: The structural primary balance is projected at -0.9% of gross domestic product on average for 2026-2035. The fiscal consolidation space is 92% in the baseline scenario .
  • C3*, context: The general government deficit is forecast to widen from 3.4% of gross domestic product in 2025 to 4.5% in 2026 and 4.6% in 2027. Expenditure growth in 2026 is driven by the delivery of F-35 jets, increasing interest payments, and higher compensation of employees due to recent wage agreements. Revenue growth is supported by a mild economic expansion but limited by weak labor market conditions and planned personal income tax cuts. The fiscal stance is expected to be expansionary in 2026 .

  • R1-E, stock-flow adjustments: The projected increase in debt is also explained by positive stock-flow adjustments (1.3% of gross domestic product on average over 2027-2036), mostly due to the build-up of a public pension fund .
  • R1-E, components missing: Data not explicitly detailed in country summary, but included in aggregate tables .

R1-T Financing behavior shifts

  • R1-T, financing behavior shifts: Defense spending has risen rapidly from close to 1.5% to around 2.4% of gross domestic product in 2024 and is targeted to increase to 3% of gross domestic product by 2029. This rise in expenditure contributes to the widening deficit and debt accumulation .

R1-A Part of the debt not exposed to the market

  • R1-A, investor base: Mitigating factors include relatively stable financing sources with a diversified and large investor base, and debt denominated exclusively in euros .

Other refinancing context

  • R1*, context: Gross general government debt is projected to rise from 85.6% of gross domestic product in 2023 to 96.3% in 2025. Debt on the Maastricht definition rose to 82.1% in 2023 and is projected to reach 88.0% in 2025 .
  • R1*, context: The document notes that household indebtedness has eased recently, with the household credit-to-gross domestic product ratio declining from 58.5% in 2020 to 51.3% in Q2 2025, as households amortised loans more than they drew new ones .
  • **R1*, context**: Public investment is expected to increase significantly in 2026–2027 due to defence equipment purchases (e.g., F35 fighter jets), raising total public investment above 5% of gross domestic product .
  • R1*, context: General government debt increased from 82.4% of gross domestic product at the end of 2024 to 88.5% of gross domestic product at the end of 2025. The increase reflects a persistent deficit, a large positive stock-flow adjustment, and modest nominal gross domestic product growth. Debt is projected to reach 91.2% of gross domestic product by the end of 2026 and 93.1% by the end of 2027 .
  • R1*, stock-flow adjustments: The document notes that the increase in the debt ratio in 2025 was mainly due to a persistent government deficit and a large positive stock-flow adjustment. In 2026, the stock-flow adjustment is expected to play only a marginal role .
  • R1*, context: Government gross financing needs are expected to recede slightly to around 19% of gross domestic product on average in 2026-2027, approaching 23% of gross domestic product in 2036 .
  • R1*, context: Gross financing needs were 20.1% of gross domestic product in 2025. Debt is projected to increase to 113.9% of gross domestic product by 2036, crossing the 90% threshold .
  • R1*, context: Public debt is projected to continue rising, reaching 91.2% of gross domestic product in 2026 and 93.1% in 2027, due to persistent large deficits .

Market Signals

  • Outlook Factors, factor decoupling: Consumer and business confidence remain subdued, particularly in the construction sector, despite a slight improvement in business confidence. House prices have stabilized after earlier declines, with the real price index of old dwellings showing limited improvement .
  • Context: Financial markets’ perceptions of sovereign risk have remained broadly stable, except for a downgrade by Fitch in July 2025 to the third highest rating, ‘AA’. The 10-year sovereign yield spread is 35 basis points versus the German Bund as of December 31, 2025 .

Outlook Material

  • Outlook Factors, geopolitical shocks: Finland faces significant security risks from Russia, including cyber-attacks and infrastructure sabotage. The country closed its border with Russia indefinitely in April 2024 and joined the North Atlantic Treaty Organization in April 2023. These factors contribute to economic uncertainty and defense spending increases .
  • Outlook Factors, demographic trends: The working-age population is projected to shrink in the 2030s under baseline migration assumptions, although recent high migration levels have led to upward revisions in population projections. Net inward migration of 40,000 annually from 2026 is assumed in the high population scenario, which would stabilize public debt at around 110% of gross domestic product from 2035 onwards .
  • Geopolitics and Defence: Finland is increasing defence expenditure to 3% of gross domestic product by 2029, up from 2.4% in 2024. The 2026 budget includes EUR 6 billion in new procurement for defence materiel. The Council of the EU has made financial assistance of up to EUR 1 billion available through the Security Action for Europe instrument .
  • Social Stability: High unemployment (10.3% in late 2025) and reforms to the social security system are raising concerns about poverty risks among vulnerable groups. The social security reforms, effective in 2026, tighten conditions and may increase labour market participation but could also disproportionately affect low-income families and young people .
  • Innovation and Productivity: Commercialisation of innovation remains problematic, with declining cooperation between academia and businesses. Scaling-up innovative firms is hampered by a lack of large-scale domestic venture capital funding for later-stage rounds .
