Slovenia

Modified

September 23, 2026

Overview

This dossier draws on the Country Report and the recommendation for Country-Specific Recommendations issued by the European Commission on 2026-06-03, the Debt Sustainability Monitor issued on 2026-02-12, the Economic Forecast issued on 2026-05-21, and the Economic Survey issued by the OECD on 2026-06-16. The evidence highlights economic growth below the euro-area average in 2025 due to weak exports and a new global energy shock, alongside tight labor markets and high wage growth that erode competitiveness. Fiscal policy faces headwinds from rising public sector wages, defense spending, and reconstruction efforts, leading to projected deficits above the 3% threshold. Structural reforms in pensions and healthcare were adopted to ensure long-term sustainability, though long-term fiscal sustainability risks remain high. Key risks include climate vulnerability, labor shortages, and the state’s significant role in the banking sector. The OECD corroborates the fiscal expansion and growth projections, while highlighting structural challenges in tax efficiency and competition enforcement.

Economic

C1* Growth (Growth)

C1-E Broken or dominated sample

  • C1-E, Broken or dominated sample: The labour market remains tight with unemployment at a historical low (3.9% in 2025), but structural challenges persist, including skill shortages and low participation of older workers. The employment rate for those aged 20-64 is 78.3%, above the European Union average.

C1-T Structural break in trend

  • C1-T, structural break in trend: Medium-term growth is projected above the euro-area average (1.9% in 2026, 2.3% in 2027), driven by robust consumption and investments. However, growth in 2025 was slower (1.1%) due to export weakness and private investment uncertainty. Labor productivity remains at 85.8% of the EU average, indicating gradual convergence.
  • C1-T, structural break: GDP is forecast to increase by 1.9% in 2026 and 2.3% in 2027, driven by domestic demand.
  • C1-T, structural break in trend: The OECD projects real GDP growth to strengthen from 1.1% in 2025 to 1.9% in 2026 and 2.2% in 2027, driven by domestic demand, resilient real incomes, and public spending on post-flood reconstruction. This contrasts with the recent slowdown caused by trade uncertainty and energy prices.

C1-A1 Growth not thin-tailed or serially independent

  • C1-A1, growth not thin-tailed or serially independent: Slovenia is exposed to rare disasters such as energy price spikes and supply chain disruptions due to its high openness (exports 78.6% of GDP) and dependence on fossil fuel imports.

C1-A2 Inherited competitiveness gap

  • C1-A2, inherited competitiveness gap: Unit labor costs increased by 5.7% in 2025, outpacing the euro-area average of 3.2%. Real effective exchange rate appreciated in 2025. Wage growth remained high at 7.1%, supported by public sector increases and competition for skilled labor. This has eroded price and cost competitiveness, particularly for exporters.
  • C1-A2, inherited competitiveness gap: Strong nominal wage growth, particularly in the public sector due to a wage reform and minimum wage indexation, is driving inflation and potentially eroding external competitiveness. The OECD notes that minimum wage increases have pushed up nominal wages considerably, with the statutory minimum wage at 60.8% of the median wage in 2024.

Other growth context

  • **C1*, context**: Structural challenges weigh on the business environment, including labour and skills shortages, regulatory and tax burdens, and lengthy permitting processes, which act as constraints on investment and growth.
  • **C1*, context**: Administrative burden is reported as a constraint by 67% of companies.
  • **C1*, context**: Public expenditure on research and development lags behind the EU average (0.64% of GDP vs 0.72%) and the national target (1.25%).
  • **C1*, context**: Business spending in R&D has fallen for three years to 1.5% of GDP.
  • **C1*, context**: The private equity and venture capital sectors are significantly less mature than EU counterparts, with assets at 0.04% and 0.01% of GDP in 2024, respectively.
  • **C1*, context**: Pension fund assets are low at 7.1% of GDP in 2024, the third-lowest level in the EU.
  • **C1*, context**: Fossil fuels constitute 58% of the energy mix, contributing to price volatility.
  • **C1*, context**: Permitting procedures for grid-scale renewable energy installations remain a major bottleneck, with environmental and municipal-level procedures lasting up to several years.
  • **C1*, context**: Deployment of new solar photovoltaic capacity slowed significantly in 2025 compared to previous years.
  • **C1*, context**: Persistent labour shortages and skills mismatches undermine investment and competitiveness, with around half of companies reporting staff shortages.
  • **C1*, context**: Adult participation in training remains low at 26.5% (EU average 39.5%).
  • **C1*, context**: Slovenia relies on foreign workers, who represented 16% of the workforce in 2025 and are projected to reach 26% by 2030.

Fiscal

C3* Fiscal capacity and plan (Fiscal Capacity)

C3-E Estimator fails or rests on one episode

  • C3-E, estimator fails or rests on one episode: Medium-term fiscal sustainability is challenged by permanently higher defence spending (planned increase to 3% of GDP by 2030) and ageing-related costs. Without further consolidation, public debt is projected to rise significantly in the long term.

C3-T History lags policy

  • C3-T, history lags policy: Slovenia adopted structural reforms in pensions, healthcare, and long-term care in 2025/2026 to ensure long-term fiscal sustainability. The pension reform raises the retirement age and adjusts accrual rates. Healthcare and long-term care laws were amended to improve accessibility and fiscal sustainability. These reforms are projected to generate savings of 1.8% of GDP by 2040.
  • C3-T, history lags policy: The Council of the EU activated the ‘national escape clause’ for Slovenia to facilitate higher defense spending, which is projected to reach 1.6% of GDP in 2026, with an objective to reach 3% by 2030. This adds to budgetary pressures alongside rising public sector wages and post-flood reconstruction.
  • C3-T, history lags policy: The government deficit is projected to widen to 3.3% of GDP in 2026 and 3.5% in 2027.
  • C3-T, history lags policy: The fiscal stance is set to become more expansionary. The general government deficit widened to 2.5% of GDP in 2025, driven by post-flood reconstruction, a public sector wage reform, and a new winter holiday allowance. The OECD projects the deficit to reach 3.3% of GDP in 2026, breaching the 3% Maastricht threshold.
  • C3-T, history lags policy: The public sector wage reform, implemented from 2025, is estimated to increase the public wage bill by 1.5 percentage points of GDP by 2028 compared to 2024, adding 0.7% of GDP to the annual fiscal deficit.

Other fiscal context

  • **C3*, context**: The structural primary balance is projected at -1.3% of GDP on average for 2026-2035. The fiscal consolidation space is 60% in the baseline scenario.
  • **C3*, context**: The SPB is assumed to be a deficit of 1.3% of GDP as of 2026, excluding changes in the cost of ageing. This level is low compared with past fiscal performance.
  • **C3*, context**: Long-term fiscal sustainability risks are high. The S2 indicator signals that Slovenia would need to improve its structural primary balance by 6.5 percentage points to ensure debt stabilizes over the long term.
  • **C3*, context**: The S1 indicator shows that a fiscal effort of 4.9 percentage points would be needed to reduce debt to 60% of GDP by 2070.
  • **C3*, context**: The general government deficit is projected to rise to 3.3% of GDP in 2026 and 3.5% of GDP in 2027. Public debt is expected to decrease slightly to 64.9% in 2026 and then increase to 65.1% in 2027. The tax mix relies heavily on labor taxes (52% of total tax revenues), with limited use of recurrent property taxes.
  • **C3*, context**: The general government deficit increased from 0.9% of GDP in 2024 to 2.5% of GDP in 2025, projected to rise to 3.3% in 2026 and 3.5% in 2027.
  • **C3*, context**: The deficit increase in 2025 reflects a permanent rise in current expenditures, record-high public investment, and a cyclical revenue slowdown.
  • **C3*, context**: The 2026 deficit increase is driven by higher current expenditure (government intermediate consumption, social benefits, public wages).
  • **C3*, context**: The 2027 deficit increase reflects the expiry of EU transfers from Recovery and Resilience Facility grants against sustained high public investments.
  • **C3*, context**: Additional deficit-increasing measures adopted in May 2026 (personal income tax, social contributions, etc.) are estimated to cost between 0.5% and 1.0% of GDP in 2026, with most measures expected to be permanent.
  • **C3*, context**: The fiscal stance was expansionary by 1.6% of GDP in 2025, projected to be expansionary by 1.3% in 2026, and contractionary by 0.7% in 2027.
  • **C3*, context**: Net expenditure grew by 8.8% in 2025 and is projected to grow by 7.2% in 2026, both above the recommended maximum growth rates.
  • **C3*, context**: Cumulative net expenditure deviation from recommended growth rates (2024-2026) is projected at 1.7% of GDP, or 1.2% of GDP when accounting for the national escape clause flexibility for defence spending.
  • **C3*, context**: Defence expenditure amounted to 1.3% of GDP in 2025 and is projected at 1.6% in 2026, reflecting an increase of 0.2 and 0.5 percentage points of GDP compared to 2021, respectively.
  • **C3*, context**: Fiscal costs of untargeted energy price mitigation measures are projected at 0.2% of GDP in 2026 (0.3% if measures last until end-2026).
  • **C3*, context**: The tax mix relies heavily on labour taxation (52% of total tax revenues in 2023), posing structural risks.
  • **C3*, context**: Tax expenditures amount to 5.2% of GDP in 2023, lacking a framework for economic efficiency evaluation.
  • **C3*, context**: Public spending efficiency is challenged by rising ageing costs; social protection spending is 17.1% of GDP, healthcare 8%, economic affairs 5.7%, and education 5.5%.

R1-A Part of the debt not exposed to the market

Other refinancing context

  • **R1*, context**: Government gross financing needs are expected to remain low, at around 8% of GDP over 2026-2027, reaching around 12% of GDP in 2036.
  • **R1*, context**: Gross financing needs were 7.7% of GDP in 2025. Debt is projected to increase to 75.1% of GDP by 2036.
  • **R1*, context**: Public debt stood at 65.7% of GDP at the end of 2025. The debt trajectory is projected to be stable-to-downward in the short term but faces pressures from rising interest costs and structural spending increases.
  • **R1*, context**: General government debt decreased from 66.4% of GDP at the end of 2024 to 65.7% at the end of 2025, projected to decrease to 64.9% in 2026 and 65.1% in 2027.
  • **R1*, context**: The debt decrease in 2025 and 2026 is mainly driven by a debt-reducing interest-growth-rate differential, with a debt-reducing stock-flow adjustment also contributing in 2026.

Bank–sovereign nexus and captive creditors

  • Bank-Sovereign Nexus: The banking sector is dominated by the state-controlled NLB and foreign-owned banks. Non-performing loans remain low (1.9% in Q3 2025), but asset quality outlook is uncertain due to geopolitical conflicts. Banks have a substantial excess of deposits over loans (loan-to-deposit ratio 74.3%), ensuring stable funding.

Willingness to pay and political stability

  • Political Stability: The document mentions that political instability is not a current red flag, but notes challenges in implementing reforms and integrating foreign workers.
  • Political stability: The outgoing government is preparing savings measures in response to recently adopted deficit-increasing measures, but uncertainty remains regarding their size and adoption.
  • Political stability and rule of law: The OECD highlights remaining challenges in the anti-corruption framework, including lobbying oversight, whistleblower protection, and the independence of investigators and prosecutors. No foreign bribery cases have been reported or investigated.

Outlook Material

  • Climate Risks: Slovenia is highly vulnerable to climate-related risks, particularly floods and heatwaves. The 2023 floods caused EUR 10 billion in damage (16% of GDP). Insurance coverage for natural disasters is among the lowest in the EU.
  • Labor Shortages: Persistent labor shortages, particularly in healthcare, education, and ICT, hinder growth and competitiveness. Skills mismatches and low digital skills among adults are significant challenges.
  • Energy policy: Slovenia adopted untargeted fiscal measures to mitigate high energy prices (excise duty and environmental tax reductions), with costs projected at 0.2-0.3% of GDP in 2026.
  • Geopolitical shocks: The economy is exposed to external shocks from the conflict in the Middle East and rising trade protectionism. Slovenia’s highly open economy relies heavily on foreign demand and inputs, particularly in the automotive and chemical sectors.
